Research

How physiological state reshapes neural circuits — measured brain-wide, then tested circuit by circuit.

The same input does not produce the same output in a different body. Hunger changes what tastes good; a reproductive state changes pain thresholds; chronic drug exposure changes how the brain answers the next dose. Across my training I have kept returning to one question in different forms: what are the rules by which internal state rewires the circuits that convert a signal into a behavior?

01

Brain-wide mapping of opioid and withdrawal states

Opioid exposure and withdrawal reorganize activity across the entire brain, but almost all of what we know comes from a handful of pre-selected regions. I generated a cellular-resolution, whole-brain c-Fos map across acute, chronic, early- and late-withdrawal, re-exposure and control conditions in both sexes, and cross-referenced it against transcriptomic atlases to recover cell-type markers and align μ-, δ- and κ-receptor expression with condition-specific activity.

02

Internal state gating of motivated behavior

Motivation is not a constant. Working in the medial preoptic area, I found that inhibitory neurons encode a post-ejaculatory negative-feedback signal that acutely suppresses female sexual drive — a clean case of a transient internal state gating a motivated behavior, captured with freely moving in vivo calcium imaging in a self-paced assay.

03

Labeled-line circuits for innate social behavior

In my doctoral work I showed that a single chemosensory cue is routed into opposite, sex-appropriate behaviors through a sexually dimorphic amygdala–hypothalamus circuit that is both necessary and sufficient. The principle — that state determines how a fixed input is converted — is the same one I now pursue for opioids.

04

Methods for reading whole-brain data

Brain-wide datasets break conventional statistics: thousands of regions, strong anatomical nesting, and sparse activation in exactly the small regions that matter. I develop the analysis that this data demands — a hierarchical multiple-testing framework that exploits the brain's nested anatomy instead of treating regions as unrelated units, and an activity-to-gene-expression analysis that links regional activity back to molecular identity.
